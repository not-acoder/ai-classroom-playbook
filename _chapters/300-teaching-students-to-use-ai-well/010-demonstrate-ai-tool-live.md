## Intent

Students see how an instructor prompts, evaluates, and edits AI output before being asked to do it themselves.


## Problem

Students use generative AI privately, and instructors rarely show their own use. Students therefore develop prompting and evaluation habits by trial and error, with no exposure to expert judgment about when output is good enough, what is worth keeping, and what should be discarded. The instructor's evaluation process, which is the actual skill, stays invisible.


## Solution

The instructor demonstrates one tool live in class, projecting the interface so students see both the prompt and the response, and shows flawed results alongside useful ones. The instructor thinks aloud: what did I ask, why am I editing this. Students then analyse the output's strengths and weaknesses and how the prompt shaped the result, and can propose improved prompts to run live. Done early in a term, it establishes norms for the course.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ The opening weeks of a course, where it sets expectations before any AI involved assignment
+ Courses about to assign work involving AI use
+ Any class with a projector and an instructor willing to show imperfect results

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Output is non-deterministic, so a live demonstration can fail in ways you did not plan for
+ Requires the instructor to be visibly uncertain in front of students, which not everyone finds comfortable
+ Takes class time that competes with content

Not a good fit when:

+ The instructor is not yet fluent enough with the tool to model evaluation credibly


## How to Implement

1. Choose one tool and one bounded task connected to course content.
2. Project the interface so students can see the prompt and the response.
3. Model how to craft a clear, bounded prompt, and tell students the goal is exploration rather than perfection.
4. Show both useful and flawed results, and discuss your thinking aloud: what did I ask, why did I edit this output.
5. Lead the class in assessing the output against criteria such as accuracy, bias, originality, tone, and alignment with the assignment.
6. Have students propose improved prompts and re-run them live.
7. In the same session, set boundaries and invitations: clarify which uses are encouraged in this course, such as brainstorming, translation, or accessibility support, and which are restricted, such as full text substitution.


## Examples

The source positions the demonstration as the first stage of a four stage cycle for student AI use, where the instructor demonstrates the tool and models prompting before students generate anything themselves.

Its instructor guidance is to model openness by showing both the useful and the flawed results and discussing the reasoning aloud, and to normalise the emotional reaction, telling students it is reasonable to feel both excited and uneasy because these literacies grow through that tension.

The source also directs instructors to integrate reflection throughout a project rather than only at the end, adding a short reflection checkpoint at ideation, drafting, revision, and presentation.
