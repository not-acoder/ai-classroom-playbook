## Intent

Students evaluate AI generated work against disciplinary standards and are assessed on the critique rather than the artefact.


## Problem

Students encounter AI output constantly and are largely unequipped to judge its quality, accuracy, or rhetorical character. Treating AI purely as something to detect leaves that gap unaddressed while consuming instructor effort on enforcement. At the same time, assignments asking students to produce text that a model can also produce no longer measure what they were designed to measure.


## Solution

The instructor makes AI output the object of study rather than the deliverable. Students actively analyse the tool's strengths and limitations, evaluate generated text for accuracy, rhetoric, and grammar, and work with multiple generated versions of the same text so they can compare across them. Exploring the tool alongside students opens a route into discussing ethics, plagiarism, and misinformation, and shifts the exercise away from shallow engagement with the material.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Writing intensive courses, where rhetoric and style are already objects of study
+ Courses where evaluating evidence and argument is an existing learning objective
+ Introductory courses where AI literacy is worth explicit attention

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Requires an evaluable artefact, which the instructor must generate or have students generate
+ Output varies between runs, so students may end up critiquing substantially different material unless the instructor supplies a common text
+ The exercise surfaces the tool's weaknesses, which can read as advocacy against AI rather than for judgment

Not a good fit when:

+ Students lack enough domain knowledge to spot errors, in which case critique becomes guesswork


## How to Implement

Steps 1, 2, and 6 are drafted. The source describes this practice at the level of assignment design rather than operational steps.

1. _(Drafted)_ Choose a course topic where weaknesses in AI output would be visible to students at their current level.
2. _(Drafted)_ Generate the artefact yourself so the whole class works from a common text, or have students generate their own if variation is acceptable.
3. Design the assignment so students actively analyse the tool's strengths and limitations rather than simply reacting to the output.
4. Generate multiple versions of the same written text so students can evaluate and critique rhetoric and grammar across versions and become familiar with different writing styles.
5. Explore the tool together with students, using the exercise to introduce the importance of ethics and the dangers of plagiarism.
6. _(Drafted)_ Assess the student's critique rather than the AI's output. See Play 29 for rubric criteria that make the judgment gradeable.


## Examples

The source is a teaching centre overview and does not supply worked implementations. It describes two forms concretely: writing assignments in which students analyse a tool's strengths and limitations, and the generation of multiple versions of one written text so students can critique rhetoric and grammar and compare writing styles. It notes the process can reduce shallow learning and raise awareness about misinformation.

Two adjacent structures appear in other verified sources and can be borrowed. WICHE describes cross-checking AI outputs against primary sources and presenting an evidence map showing what was confirmed or contradicted, and describes side by side annotation activities comparing an AI generated draft with the student's human revision.

This Examples section is thin because of what the primary source contains, not because the practice lacks detail elsewhere. See Appendix A.
