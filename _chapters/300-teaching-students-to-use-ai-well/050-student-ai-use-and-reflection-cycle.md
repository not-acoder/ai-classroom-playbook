## Intent

Students document how they used AI on an assignment and explain what they kept, changed, or rejected.


## Problem

When AI use is invisible, the instructor can assess only the final artefact, which reveals little about the student's own reasoning. Students also have no structured occasion to notice how the tool shaped their thinking, so the interaction produces a document but not much learning. Prohibition does not solve this; it drives use underground and makes it undiscussable.


## Solution

Students work through a documented cycle. They use AI for an initial idea, outline, or draft while recording the exact prompts; review the output against stated criteria and annotate what seems credible and what feels wrong; revise substantially in their own voice, noting what they kept, changed, or deleted and why; and reflect on the interaction. The submitted artefacts are the prompt record, the annotated output, a side by side comparison, and the reflection. Assessment weight sits on the depth of the reasoning rather than on whether AI was used.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Writing intensive and project courses where process already carries assessment weight
+ Courses where a process portfolio can replace a single artefact submission
+ Courses with multilingual students, where AI use

for language support benefits from structured attribution

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Adds submission overhead for students and reading overhead for the instructor
+ Reflections become performative if students believe a particular answer is wanted, so the criteria must genuinely reward honest reporting of failure
+ The documentation can exceed the work if the assignment is short

Not a good fit when:

+ AI use is prohibited in the course
+ The assignment is too small to justify the process artefacts


## How to Implement

1. Demonstrate one tool and model a clear, bounded prompt before assigning the cycle. See Play 9.
2. Use: students generate an initial idea, outline, or draft with AI and record the exact prompts used and what they noticed. The artefact is a screenshot of the prompts and first output plus a short note.
3. Review: give students an evaluation checklist covering accuracy, bias, originality, tone, and alignment with the assignment. Students assess the output against it and highlight what seems credible, what feels wrong, and where human judgment is needed. The artefact is the annotated output.
4. Revise: students produce a human revised version that keeps useful ideas but rewrites or expands them, and write a one paragraph note on what they kept, changed, or deleted and why. The artefact is a side by side comparison.
5. Reflect: students complete reflection prompts and join a peer discussion.
6. Integrate reflection checkpoints throughout the project rather than only at the end: at ideation, drafting, revision, and presentation.
7. Grade on depth of analysis, not on the presence or absence of AI use. See Play 29 for rubric criteria.


## Examples

Reflection prompts from the source: what question or problem did you bring to the AI; what did you learn about your own process from the interaction; where did the AI frustrate or mislead you; what creative idea or shortcut did it reveal that you might keep using; how did using AI change your confidence or curiosity about this topic.

A discussion format from the source: in small groups, each student shares one AI win and one AI fail, then groups identify one insight to post to a shared board organised around when AI helps learning, when it hurts learning, and when it surprises.

A submission level variant from the source: replace a single essay submission with a process portfolio containing prompt histories, reflections, and revision rationales, or require an editor's memo explaining one key decision the student made based on AI feedback.

A field example from the source: at Cochise College, an ESL instructor redesigned writing assignments so students used AI for brainstorming and grammar support with structured attribution and reflection, sharing prompt histories and process notes in collaborative documents. The instructor reported more nuanced conversations about voice and academic integrity, and the model spread to other language support and developmental writing courses.
