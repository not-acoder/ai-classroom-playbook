## Intent

Before an instructor requires students to use an AI tool, or uploads student work to one, the tool's data handling has been checked against institutional policy.


## Problem

Requiring a tool is requiring a student to hand their data to a company. Most AI tools are consumer products whose terms grant the provider rights over submitted content, and a student who objects has no route to opt out of a required assignment. The same exposure runs the other way when an instructor uploads student writing for summarisation or feedback drafting, which is student work leaving the institution without the student's involvement. The check is quick and almost never performed, because the tool is free and the friction of using it is zero, which is exactly why the question does not come up.


## Solution

The instructor checks three things before requiring a tool or uploading student work: whether the tool complies with institutional data privacy policy, whether an institutional account exists and can be used instead of a personal one, and what the terms of service say about data ownership and use. Where the check fails, the instructor uses a different tool or removes the requirement.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Any course about to require a tool for the first time
+ Courses running Play 4, where student writing is uploaded for summarisation
+ Institutions with a licensed tool, where the check usually resolves to use the licensed one

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Terms of service change without notice, so a check performed once is a check performed for one term
+ The instructor may not be qualified to interpret the terms and should route genuine uncertainty to institutional IT or legal rather than deciding alone
+ An institutional licence resolves the compliance question and not the underlying question of whether student work should be processed by a model at all

Not a good fit when:

+ Nothing. This is a precondition rather than an option


## How to Implement

Steps 4 and 5 are drafted.

1. Verify that the tool complies with your institution's data privacy policies before requiring it.
2. Use institutional accounts rather than personal ones wherever possible.
3. Check the terms of service regarding data ownership and usage.
4. _(Drafted)_ Apply the same check before you upload student work yourself. Play 4 involves sending student writing to an external tool and is the common case.
5. _(Drafted)_ If the check fails and no compliant alternative exists, drop the requirement rather than asking students to accept the terms individually. A student cannot meaningfully decline a term they need to pass the course.


## Examples

Whitman lists three security and privacy considerations under tool selection: verify that tools comply with your institution's data privacy policies, use institutional accounts rather than personal ones when possible, and check terms of service regarding data ownership and usage.

WICHE places the same concern at the level of institutional literacy rather than individual compliance, describing the capacity to build transparency into decisions about data, privacy, and tool selection as one of the things AI literacies are for.

Neither source supplies a checklist or names specific tools as compliant, which is appropriate since compliance is institution specific.
