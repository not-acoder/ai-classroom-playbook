## Intent

No student is advantaged or disadvantaged on a graded task by which AI tools they can afford.


## Problem

Generative AI has a paid tier and a free tier, and they do not perform equally. A course that permits AI use without specifying which tools converts a difference in household income into a difference in output quality, silently and on graded work. The students affected are the ones for whom other course costs are already binding, and unlike a textbook the disparity is invisible to the instructor because both students submit a document. Requiring students to compare multiple tools, as in Play 13, sharpens the problem rather than avoiding it.


## Solution

The instructor specifies which tool or tools the graded task assumes, and ensures every student can reach them: an institutionally licensed tool where one exists, or a free tier tool named explicitly so that nobody is guessing. Where the task genuinely requires a paid capability, the instructor supplies the output or provides an alternative path rather than assuming purchase.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Any course where AI use on graded work is permitted
+ Institutions with a licensed tool, where the fix is to name it
+ Courses with a mixed intake where the cost difference is material

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Naming one tool constrains the exercise and removes the tool choice that Play 13 is built on. The two plays trade against each other and the instructor has to choose per assignment
+ Free tiers change without notice, so the specified path needs checking each term
+ Equal access to a tool is not equal capability with it, and this play does not address the second

Not a good fit when:

+ No AI use is permitted on graded work, in which case the problem does not arise


## How to Implement

Steps 2, 4, and 5 are drafted.

1. Provide access to the same AI tools for all students on any graded task, which was the equity recommendation students themselves produced in the source study.
2. _(Drafted)_ Name the specific tool and tier in the assignment brief rather than saying an AI tool, since ambiguity is what lets the disparity in.
3. Use an institutionally licensed tool where one exists, and use institutional accounts rather than personal ones. This also addresses the data question in Play 28.
4. _(Drafted)_ Where a task needs a capability only a paid tier provides, supply the output yourself or provide an alternative path. Do not assume purchase.
5. _(Drafted)_ Recheck the named free tier at the start of each term, since capability and limits move.


## Examples

The equity provision in this play comes from students rather than from faculty. In the participatory

policy study behind Play 16, ten graduate students produced ten policy recommendations, the tenth of which was ensuring equity by providing access to the same AI tools for all students. That it appeared in a student authored list and not in the faculty guidance the same study reviewed is the finding.

McGee and Sadler examined the free and paid versions of the same assistant on homework questions and data analyses typical of an introductory statistics course, framing the question directly as whether students who cannot afford newer and faster versions would be disadvantaged in writing such projects and learning these methods. Their framing is the useful part. Their comparison is between model generations that no longer correspond to the current free and paid tiers, and the paper is an unrefereed preprint whose arXiv record states it was submitted for review and rejected. Cite the question, not the result.

Whitman's guidance on tool selection covers the institutional angle: take advantage of educational pricing and institutional licences, and consider free tiers for initial experimentation.
