## Intent

Students know, before beginning any assignment, which uses of AI are permitted in the course and what they are required to disclose.


## Problem

Students arrive already using generative AI, and without explicit course guidance they are left to guess where the line falls. Ambiguity produces both accidental integrity violations and students avoiding uses the instructor would have permitted. A single blanket rule does not fit every discipline: what counts as augmenting learning in a business course differs from what counts in a humanities writing seminar, and the same tool use can be appropriate in one and corrosive in the other. It also does not fit every assessment within one course.


## Solution

The instructor publishes a policy specifying which AI uses are permitted, which are restricted, and what disclosure is required, and sets the level per assessment rather than once for the whole course. The AI Assessment Scale provides a ready structure for this, running from no AI, through AI planning and AI collaboration, to full AI and AI exploration. The underlying distinction the instructor has to draw for their own discipline is between uses that augment learning and uses that replace it, which is a judgment no scale makes for you.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Any course, and particularly programmes wanting consistency across multiple sections
+ Disciplines where the line between augmenting and replacing learning is discipline specific and needs stating
+ Courses with varied assessment types, where one policy would be wrong for most of them

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ A policy is a discursive change in the sense of Play 24. It communicates a rule and does not enforce it. Pair it with structural changes rather than treating it as sufficient on its own
+ Different policies across courses can confuse students unless there is a shared institutional vocabulary
+ Policies age quickly as tool capability changes and need revisiting
+ A per assessment scale takes more thought to write than a blanket prohibition

Not a good fit when:

+ The institution mandates a single policy and does not permit instructor variation


## How to Implement

Step 5 is drafted.

1. Decide the course's overall stance. Stances can span the whole range from allowing AI for all learning tasks with attribution to not allowing it under any circumstances.
2. Set a level per assessment rather than one level for the course. The AI Assessment Scale supplies five: no AI, AI planning, AI collaboration, full AI, and AI exploration.
3. Write out which specific uses fall on each side of the augment and replace line for your discipline, rather than leaving the categories abstract.
4. State what disclosure requires: when, how, and why a tool was used. See Play 23 for a standardised format.
5. _(Drafted)_ Pair the policy with at least one structural change, since a policy alone relies on compliance you cannot verify.
6. Add the statement to your syllabus and course site, specifying which tools students may use, what types of uses are allowed, and how students should cite or acknowledge AI use.
7. Discuss the policy during the first class and revisit it throughout the semester, explaining your rationale and making it a discussion topic.
8. Review the statement for clarity, looking for gaps to address and ambiguous wording to revise.
9. Revisit the policy as new tools and capabilities emerge.


## Examples

The AI Assessment Scale was revised in 2024 and again in 2025 on the basis of implementation feedback from global adoption across K-12 and higher education. The revision explicitly moved away from the traffic light visual of the original in favour of a neutral palette, on the grounds that the traffic light framing carried connotations the authors no longer wanted. It is designed to serve two purposes at once: to open dialogue with students about appropriate use, and to prompt the instructor to redesign the assessment.

The CHEPP report describes a course level version of the same idea. At Dallas College and the University of South Carolina, faculty choose one of three AI policies for each course they teach: a red light option prohibiting any use of AI, a yellow light option allowing AI for specified purposes with clear and transparent citation, and a green light option allowing any use that augments rather than replaces learning, with citation. The AI Assessment Scale makes the same choice per assessment rather than per course. The report also cites survey data in which 97 percent of surveyed learners said institutions should adopt clearer AI policies (Flaherty, 2025, as cited in the report).

Discipline specific illustrations from the CHEPP report, drawn from learner focus groups rather than documented implementations: business courses may encourage AI for any task that creates efficiencies valued in a corporate environment; statistics courses may allow AI for analysing large datasets and computer science courses may allow it as a round the clock tutor for difficult coding projects; humanities courses may prohibit AI entirely to ensure students perform the critical thinking and analysis that accompany reading and writing assignments.

Whitman's version of the same policy lists four things to state: how you use AI in your teaching practice, guidelines for student use, expectations for attribution and transparency, and assessment considerations. The first of those is Play 26.
