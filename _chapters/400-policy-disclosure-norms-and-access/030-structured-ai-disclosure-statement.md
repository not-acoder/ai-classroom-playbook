## Intent

Students append a standardised statement to their work naming which AI tools they used and at which stages, in a consistent format across the course.


## Problem

Where disclosure is required at all it is usually requested in free form, so students guess at scope and detail and produce statements that are not comparable with each other. A student who used a model to brainstorm and a student who used one to draft may write disclosures of similar length and vagueness. Citation conventions do not solve this, because a citation records that a source was consulted while disclosure needs to record what the tool did at which point in the work.


## Solution

The instructor adopts a standard disclosure format with defined headings corresponding to stages of the work, and requires students to include only the headings for stages where AI was actually used. The Artificial Intelligence Disclosure framework provides such a format, appended at the end of the work in the manner of an acknowledgements section, naming the tools used and the manner of use at each point of engagement. Because the headings are fixed, statements become comparable, and because unused headings are omitted, an honest minimal disclosure stays short.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses at AI collaboration level or above in the sense of Play 6, where use is permitted and needs recording
+ Research methods and capstone courses, where the format transfers directly to the students' later scholarly writing
+ Programmes wanting one disclosure convention across many courses

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ A disclosure statement is a discursive instrument. It records what the student says they did and cannot verify it. Do not treat it as an integrity control
+ The framework was designed for research writing, so headings may need adapting for coursework that is not a paper
+ Requiring disclosure without also permitting use produces either empty statements or dishonest ones

Not a good fit when:

+ AI use is prohibited in the course, in which case there is nothing to disclose


## How to Implement

Step 5 is drafted.

1. Adopt a standard format rather than asking for disclosure in free form, and give students the headings in advance.
2. Require the statement to be appended at the end of the work, in the manner of an acknowledgements section, naming the AI tools used and the way they were used at each stage.
3. Tell students to include only the headings for stages where AI was actually used, so a minimal honest disclosure is short rather than performative.
4. Direct students to the framework's interactive statement builder at aidframework.org if you want consistency without teaching the format from scratch. Check the framework's licence before adapting the headings for your discipline.
5. _(Drafted)_ Say explicitly how the disclosure affects the grade. If it does not affect it, say that too, since students will otherwise under-disclose defensively.


## Examples

The Artificial Intelligence Disclosure framework was developed by Kari D. Weaver, a librarian at the University of Waterloo, to provide brief, targeted disclosure about AI use based on the range of activities involved in research writing. The AID statement is appended to the end of the work, detailing the tools used and the manner of use, organised by the possible points of engagement through the writing process.

The framework's stated argument for standardisation is that a consistent approach simplifies the expectations and the elements needed to maintain academic and research ethics. It argues that a citation alone does not capture how AI was used, so AI use needs to be attributed as well.

An interactive statement builder is available at aidframework.org.

A Virginia Tech instructor found a disclosure checklist did not stop undisclosed use across four graduate courses (Kaufman, 2026). A chemistry course's disclosure forms showed most disclosing students did more than three quarters of the work with AI (Garcia Ramos, 2025).
