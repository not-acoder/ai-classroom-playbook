## Intent

Students help set the course's AI norms and can articulate the reasoning behind them.


## Problem

An AI policy handed down as a rule invites compliance at best and evasion at worst. Institutional AI policy overwhelmingly emphasises the prevention of misconduct and is frequently framed in punitive rather than pedagogical terms, and across universities one pattern holds: students' perspectives are largely absent from it. Students are the group most affected and they bring the most direct knowledge of how these tools are actually used in coursework. A policy nobody discussed also ages badly, because no one has a reason to revisit it.


## Solution

The instructor treats the policy as a teaching tool rather than as legalese, and sets permission levels through active collaboration with students. Because students are being asked to disclose their own practice to the person who grades them, the collaboration needs procedural safeguards rather than only good intentions: student led sessions, honest disclosure protected from assessment consequences, and a visible route from what students say to what the policy becomes.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Small and medium classes where genuine negotiation is feasible
+ Upper division and graduate courses where students have professional stakes in the norms
+ Courses running across multiple terms, where the policy can improve iteratively

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ The power imbalance between students and the instructor who grades them is the central design problem, not a footnote. Without safeguards the exercise produces the answers students think you want
+ Students may propose norms the instructor cannot accept, so the boundaries of the negotiation must be clear from the start
+ Consumes class time early in the term
+ Co-created norms can drift from institutional or departmental requirements

Not a good fit when:

+ Institutional or departmental policy is fixed and leaves nothing to negotiate
+ Enrolment makes real deliberation impossible


## How to Implement

1. Decide the range you are willing to negotiate within, and say so. Policy stances can span from allowing AI for all learning tasks with attribution to prohibiting it entirely.
2. Address the power imbalance before you address the policy. Run the discussion sessions student led, without instructor oversight of the conversation itself, so students can disclose actual use and voice concerns candidly.
3. Survey students first on their existing use, skills, and opinions, so the discussion starts from their practice rather than from your assumptions.
4. Run the drafting as a structured conversation rather than a single meeting. A workable shape is a short series: candid discussion of actual use, then drafting, then refinement, with follow up interviews or written comment for students who will not speak in a group.
5. Produce a shareable artefact of the agreed norms rather than a paragraph in the syllabus. Students in the source study made a zine.
6. Add the resulting statement to your syllabus and course site, specifying which tools students may use, what uses are allowed, and how students should cite or acknowledge AI use.
7. Discuss the policy during the first class and revisit it throughout the semester, explaining your rationale and making it a discussion topic.
8. Review the statement for clarity, looking for gaps to address and ambiguous wording to revise.


## Examples

Seki, Vijay and Kotturi ran a three part participatory design workshop series with follow up interviews and ten graduate design students at a minority serving institution. They implemented explicit procedural safeguards to address the power dynamic: workshop activities were student led to foster open dialogue without faculty oversight, and the research team ensured faculty were kept out of the room. Students' top two motivations for participating were learning to use AI more ethically and having their perspectives considered in policy creation.

The students produced ten policy recommendations. Among them: instructors giving clear assignment specific AI guidelines, ownership questions, using AI to support peer feedback, and ensuring equity by providing access to the same AI tools for all students, which is Play 27. The recommendations were published as a zine, a shareable booklet, rather than as a policy document.

The Pitt source establishes the underlying move in one line: permission levels can emerge from active collaboration with your students. Its framing of why this matters is that the policy is more than a rule, it is a teaching tool communicating values about learning, integrity, and originality.
