## Intent

Students know how their instructor uses AI in teaching the course, stated before students are asked to disclose their own use.


## Problem

Instructors ask students for transparency about AI while saying nothing about their own use, which students notice. If AI drafted the rubric, generated the quiz, or produced the feedback comments, and the policy requires students to declare far less than that, the asymmetry undermines the policy it accompanies. Students also have no model of what a considered position on AI use looks like, so disclosure becomes a compliance form rather than a practice.


## Solution

The instructor states in the syllabus and in the first class how they do and do not use AI in the course, and connects those choices to their teaching values rather than presenting them as a list. The statement is made before students are asked to disclose anything, and it names limits as well as uses, since where an instructor declines to use AI is more informative than where they do.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Any course publishing an AI policy, since this is an addition to an existing document
+ Courses where the instructor uses AI substantially in preparation, where silence is the greater risk
+ Courses that ask students for detailed disclosure, where the asymmetry would otherwise be visible

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Disclosure of instructor use in a restrictive course reads as inconsistent unless the reasoning is given, and the reasoning is the point
+ The statement dates as the instructor's own practice changes across a term
+ No source evaluates the effect of instructor disclosure on student behaviour or trust

Not a good fit when:

+ Nothing. An instructor who uses no AI can say so, which is itself the disclosure


## How to Implement

Steps 3 and 5 are drafted.

1. List where AI is present in your own course preparation: objectives, rubrics, quizzes, examples, feedback templates, slides.
2. State that list in the syllabus AI policy, as one of its named components alongside guidelines for student use, expectations for attribution, and assessment considerations.
3. _(Drafted)_ Name at least one thing you decline to use AI for, and say why. A statement listing only uses reads as promotion.
4. Connect the choices to your teaching philosophy rather than presenting them as a list of tools, and share that connection with students.
5. _(Drafted)_ Make the statement before students are asked to disclose their own use, not after.
6. Discuss it in the first class alongside the policy, and revisit it when your own practice changes.


## Examples

Whitman lists implementing transparent AI policies as one of five entry points for faculty and specifies four things such a policy should outline, of which the first is how you use AI in your teaching practice. The stated rationale is that being open about AI use builds trust and models digital literacy.

WICHE describes the practice as a recurring faculty move rather than a one time statement: faculty start each semester asking students about their AI experiences and goals, design assignments reflecting disciplinary values, and share how their own AI use aligns with their teaching philosophy. The pairing matters. The instructor asks first and discloses in the same conversation.

Neither source supplies example syllabus language.
