## Intent

Students who need captioning, translation, or text simplification know these uses are permitted and are not weighing them against an integrity risk.


## Problem

Blanket AI restrictions fall unevenly. A student using a model for real time captioning, translation of course materials, or simplification of dense reading is doing something categorically different from a student generating an essay, but a policy that does not distinguish them leaves the first student uncertain whether they are cheating. The cost lands on students with disabilities and multilingual students, who are least able to absorb it.


## Solution

The instructor names accessibility and language support uses as explicitly permitted in the course policy, separately from the general permission tier, and gives concrete examples so students do not have to interpret. The framing is that these uses fall under accessible course design rather than under integrity exceptions. Where the support changes meaning, as translation does, students can be asked to note the change rather than to avoid the tool.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses with multilingual students or students using accommodations
+ Reading heavy courses where simplification and summarisation materially change access
+ Any course already writing an AI policy, since this is an addition rather than a separate document

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ The line between simplifying a reading and outsourcing engagement with it is genuinely blurry and will need judgment calls
+ Naming permitted uses invites edge cases the policy did not anticipate
+ Tool quality for translation and captioning varies by language, so the permission does not equalise access on its own. Access to the tool is also unequal; see Play 27

Not a good fit when:

+ Institutional accommodation processes already cover these uses and a course level statement would conflict


## How to Implement

Steps 4 and 5 are drafted.

1. Identify which support uses are relevant to your course materials and student population.
2. Name them explicitly as permitted in your syllabus AI policy, separate from your general permission tier. Uses that augment rather than replace learning include real time captioning and text to speech for learners with disabilities, real time translation of course materials and discussions for multilingual learners, summarising lecture notes, and improving clarity of writing.
3. Distinguish these from uses that replace learning, which include writing an essay in response to a prompt, generating answers to discussion questions, developing a research thesis, and solving assigned problems.
4. _(Drafted)_ Give students a concrete example of each side of the line for your own assignments, rather than leaving the categories abstract.
5. _(Drafted)_ Point students to a short resource on configuring the tools, since permission without setup guidance leaves the gap partly open.


## Examples

The CHEPP report frames these capabilities as part of institutional efforts toward Universal Design for Learning, providing enhanced accessibility to course materials for learners with disabilities as well as multilingual learners.

WICHE describes the student facing version and adds a productive condition: encourage students to use AI for translation or accessibility support while keeping reflective commentary about how meaning or tone shifted. That turns the support use into an occasion for language awareness rather than only a permission.

The Cochise College example in Play 10 is this play in operation. An ESL instructor redesigned writing assignments so students used AI for brainstorming and grammar support with structured attribution and reflection, and reported more nuanced conversations about voice and academic integrity.

Neither policy source supplies syllabus language. Steps 4 and 5 exist to fill that gap and are drafted.
