## Intent

The instructor identifies the class's shared points of confusion after a session and adjusts the next one accordingly.


## Problem

Classroom assessment techniques such as the one minute paper or muddiest point surface what students understood and what confused them. Reviewing every response takes time instructors do not have, and when they do read them, they tend to fixate on individual answers or interpret the set in a way that confirms how they already believe the class went. The technique's value depends on synthesis that rarely happens.


## Solution

Students submit short free text responses to a question about what mattered most and what confused them. The instructor passes the collected set to the AI and asks it to identify common themes and patterns and to list

the key points students found important alongside the main areas of confusion. The instructor checks the summary against the raw responses and adjusts the following class. The model's weakness, that it does not know your class, is also its strength: it has no stake in how the session went.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Medium and large classes where reading every response individually is impractical
+ Courses where responses can be collected through a shared document or an anonymous survey
+ Any session where the instructor has a following class in which to act on what they learn

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Students may be reluctant to reveal what they did not understand, so anonymity matters
+ A summary can flatten a small number of important outlier responses
+ Student writing is student data. Confirm before sending it to an external tool that the tool complies with your institution's data privacy policies, and prefer an institutional account. See Play 28

Not a good fit when:

+ The class is small enough to read directly
+ Institutional policy prohibits submitting student work to external AI tools


## How to Implement

1. Decide what to focus on: a specific activity, topic, or class discussion.
2. Write a question that will uncover what students understood and what confused them.
3. Collect responses in a shared document or anonymous survey.
4. Check the tool against your institution's data privacy policy before uploading student writing, and use an institutional account where one exists.
5. Prompt the AI to review the responses, identify common themes and patterns, and list the key points students found most important and the main areas of confusion.
6. Evaluate the output. Do the themes align with your goals for the class? Are the confusion points typical gaps you have seen before? If the output does not match your expectations, read the responses directly.
7. Adjust your teaching in the next class to clarify misconceptions, provide additional resources, or address the gaps.


## Examples

Question options from the source: what was the most important idea or concept covered in class today and why is it important; what is the most difficult class concept so far and what did you struggle to understand; what concept or problem would you like explored in more detail.

A dynamic variant from the source: post the question mid class rather than at the end, upload the responses, show students the AI generated output, and discuss it live. What did the AI point out, what patterns did it highlight, what confusion is shared? This can turn into students answering each other's questions.

An anonymity technique from the source: when using a discussion board, set it to reveal responses only after a student has posted their own, so students are not influenced by classmates.
