## Intent

Each assessment in the course is deliberately placed in one of two lanes, either controlled and supervised or fully open to AI use, rather than sitting in an unenforced middle.


## Problem

The common institutional response to generative AI is to communicate rules about permissible use, often through a traffic light style scheme. These changes are discursive: they modify the instructions around a task while leaving the task itself unchanged, so their validity depends entirely on students choosing to comply. The instructor has no reliable way to verify compliance, since detection tools produce false positives and false negatives at rates that make them unusable as evidence. The more precisely the rules specify acceptable use, the more visible the gap between the rule and any means of enforcing it becomes.


## Solution

The instructor stops trying to police the middle and sorts assessment into two lanes. Lane one is controlled: supervised, in person, and validity comes from the conditions. Lane two is open: no attempt is made to control AI use, and validity comes from structural features of the task that make substitution unproductive rather than forbidden. Both lanes are honest. What is removed is the unsupervised task carrying a rule nobody can enforce.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses currently relying on a permitted use policy with no enforcement behind it
+ Courses being redesigned rather than patched
+ Programmes wanting a defensible position on assessment validity

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Lane one costs supervision time and room capacity, which are institutional resources the instructor may not control
+ Sorting the whole assessment schedule is a larger commitment than adding a policy statement
+ Some tasks resist both lanes, and the framework does not resolve those

Not a good fit when:

+ No supervised assessment is available at all, in which case the course has only lane two and should say so


## How to Implement

Steps 1 and 5 are drafted.

1. _(Drafted)_ List every graded task in the course and ask of each whether its validity currently rests on a rule students could ignore.
2. Place each task in one of two lanes. Lane one is controlled, in person assessment. Lane two is open, with no attempt to control AI use.
3. For lane two tasks, build in structural features that discourage substitution rather than prohibiting it. The source's examples include requiring sign off on data produced in a lab so that students must continue working with that dataset rather than generating synthetic data, and inserting checkpoints where students discuss their work in person and then build on the discussed ideas asynchronously.
4. Distinguish structural from discursive changes as you go. A structural change alters the mechanics of the task. A discursive change alters the instructions around it.
5. _(Drafted)_ State the lane on each assignment brief, so students understand the design rather than reading lane two as permission gained by argument.


## Examples

The source's central distinction is between discursive changes, which modify instructions that students remain free to ignore, and structural changes, which reshape the underlying mechanics of the assessment task itself. It identifies the traffic light approach common in institutional policy as the paradigm case of the former.

The source names three reasons discursive approaches fail, of which the third is the most consequential for an individual instructor: they assume the educator has a reliable mechanism to verify compliance, and generative AI has undermined the traditional verification methods.

The two lane structure is the source's proposed alternative, with lane one controlled and lane two open but structurally shaped.
