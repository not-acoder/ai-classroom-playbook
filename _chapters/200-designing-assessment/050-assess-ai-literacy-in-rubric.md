## Intent

Students are graded on how well they evaluate, verify and correct AI output, not only on the quality of the artefact they submit.


## Problem

Traditional rubrics score the product. When AI can produce the product, a rubric that scores only the product rewards whatever produced it and tells the instructor nothing about the student's judgment. The instructor is then left monitoring AI use as a policing problem, which is unenforceable, rather than assessing it as a skill, which is gradeable. Meanwhile the ability to judge AI output is a competence students will need and nobody is scoring.


## Solution

The instructor adds criteria to the existing rubric that make AI engagement an assessable skill. The criteria describe levels of judgment rather than levels of use. The source does not publish its level descriptors, so the progression suggested in How to Implement is drafted. The rubric change reframes the instructor's own orientation from monitoring AI use toward teaching responsible AI practice, which is the reported effect on educators who make the switch.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses where AI use is permitted and currently unassessed
+ Professional programmes where students will use these tools in practice and should be evaluated on doing so well
+ Courses that already grade against a written rubric, since this is an addition rather than a new instrument

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ The criteria are only gradeable if the student's engagement is visible, so this play depends on a process artefact such as the cycle in Play 10 or the disclosure statement in Play 23
+ Scoring judgment quality is harder than scoring product quality and inter-rater consistency will need attention
+ In a course where AI use is prohibited the criteria are unusable

Not a good fit when:

+ No process evidence is collected, in which case there is nothing to grade the criteria against


## How to Implement

Steps 1, 3, 4, and 5 are drafted.

1. _(Drafted)_ Confirm you are collecting process evidence. Without it this play has nothing to assess.
2. Add criteria to the existing rubric that frame AI engagement explicitly as an assessable skill rather than as a compliance question.
3. _(Drafted)_ Write the performance levels as levels of judgment. A workable progression runs from accepting output uncritically, through correcting it with support, to independently evaluating output and re-prompting.
4. _(Drafted)_ Show students the criteria before the assignment, since the point is to teach the judgment rather than to catch its absence.
5. _(Drafted)_ Check after the first run whether graders are applying the new criteria consistently, which is where rubric changes usually fail.


## Examples

The source studied educators moving from a traditional to an AI aware rubric in a health and medicine course and examined the effect on grading practices, instructional strategies, and perceptions of student

engagement. Educators described a shift in their own orientation, from monitoring AI use toward teaching responsible AI practice, which they attributed to the rubric's explicit framing of AI engagement as an assessable skill.

The source is a qualitative study of educator experience in one course. It does not measure effects on student learning.
