## Intent

Students demonstrate knowledge in an unscripted one to one conversation rather than exclusively through take home artefacts.


## Problem

Take home written work can be produced entirely by a generative model, so it no longer reliably evidences a student's own understanding. Detection tools are unreliable and put the instructor in an adversarial posture toward the class. Written examinations, which remain prevalent in STEM, face separate and older criticism regarding authenticity and their capacity to assess deep learning. The alternative is to change what is being assessed rather than trying to police how the artefact was produced.


## Solution

The instructor moves graded weight onto an interactive oral assessment: a scheduled one to one conversation in which the student explains and defends their work against unscripted, individualised follow up questions. Because the questions respond to what this student says, they cannot be anticipated or pre-generated. Assessment is against standardised criteria to control for the subjectivity that oral formats are traditionally criticised for. This is a lane one move in the sense of Play 24: validity comes from the conditions, not from a rule.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses where the final assessment is currently a written examination or take home artefact
+ Disciplines where professional practice already involves defending reasoning aloud
+ Courses small enough for scheduling to be feasible, or large enough to justify a team of trained assessors

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Oral assessment consumes scheduling and staff time and scales badly without assessor training
+ Assessor consistency requires standardised criteria and training, without which the format inherits its historical reliability criticism
+ Live formats can disadvantage students with anxiety or students working in a second language unless deliberately designed around that. One study found no significant performance difference across gender, international status, or language background, which is encouraging but is a single context
+ An accessibility preserving alternative is the written viva, in which individualised probing questions drawn from the student's own process documentation are answered in writing under supervised timed conditions

Not a good fit when:

+ Enrolment makes individual oral assessment impossible without sacrificing instruction and no assessor team is available
+ The learning outcome genuinely requires extended written work that cannot be demonstrated in conversation


## How to Implement

Step 6 is drafted.

1. Identify which learning outcomes are currently assessed only through take home written work or a written examination.
2. Design the oral as a real time interactive exchange in which the student explains and defends their reasoning, not a rehearsed presentation. The distinguishing feature is that the assessor asks unscripted follow up questions responding to what the student actually says.
3. Build standardised assessment criteria before running it. This is the control for the subjectivity concern that oral assessment has historically attracted, and the point at which most implementations fail.
4. Train every assessor against those criteria if more than one person will assess, and check for assessor effects afterwards.
5. If the oral assures learning on a linked artefact such as a written report, give feedback on both components at the same time.
6. _(Drafted)_ Tell students the change supports authentic demonstration of learning rather than functioning as a punitive anti-AI measure, and give them the criteria in advance.


## Examples

Davey and colleagues replaced the major final assessment in an undergraduate bioscience course with a one to one interactive oral, evaluated across course offerings from 2009 to 2023 covering 722 students. They report significant improvement in both final assessment performance and overall course grades following its introduction, with no significant differences across gender, international status, or language background, and describe the format as resilient to academic misconduct including inappropriate use of generative AI. Assessor comparison found no statistically significant difference in mean marks between assessors, which is the standardised criteria and training working as intended.

Droulers, Krautloher and Shaeri report a multi year multi subject implementation at an Australian institution, arguing that the interactive oral resolves rather than sits between formative and summative purposes, since the conversation delivers feedback while the judgment is being made.

Sotiriadou and colleagues provide the pre-generative-AI foundation, examining the interactive oral as authentic assessment that preserves academic integrity and develops employability related skills.
