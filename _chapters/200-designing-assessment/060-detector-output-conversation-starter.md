## Intent

When AI detection software flags a student's work, the instructor opens a process conversation rather than an integrity case.


## Problem

Detection software presents a probability as though it were a finding, and instructors under time pressure act on it. The error rate makes this indefensible, and the errors are not randomly distributed. Detectors misclassify writing by non-native English speakers at rates that make the tool actively discriminatory, because they treat limited lexical variety as a signal of machine authorship. A student penalised on that basis is being penalised for their linguistic background. Separately, detection is trivially evadable by anyone who prompts for more literary language, so the tool fails hardest against the students most likely to be misusing it and hits hardest the students least likely to be.


## Solution

The instructor treats any detector output as a prompt to look at process evidence, never as evidence in itself. If a flag arises, the instructor asks the student to walk through how the work was produced: drafts, notes, sources, decisions. The conversation is framed as a discussion about process, not an accusation. If the course collects process artefacts already, the flag is usually resolved without a conversation at all. Some institutions and teaching centres go further and advise against using detectors at all, on the grounds that they create an atmosphere of mistrust.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Any course where detection software is enabled by default in the LMS and the instructor cannot switch it off
+ Courses with multilingual students, where the bias risk is concrete rather than theoretical
+ Courses already collecting process evidence, which supplies the conversation's material

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ A process conversation costs instructor time that acting on a score does not
+ Students may experience even a carefully framed conversation as an accusation, so framing and timing matter
+ Where an institution requires detector scores to be reported, the instructor may not be free to disregard them and should record their reservations

Not a good fit when:

+ Nothing here fits the case where an instructor has independent evidence of misconduct. This play addresses the detector output, not the underlying question


## How to Implement

Steps 1, 3, 4 and 5 are drafted. The evidence source documents the error rates and does not prescribe classroom procedure.

1. _(Drafted)_ Decide before the term whether you will use a detector at all. The Pitt guidance is to avoid them, on the grounds that they are unreliable and create an atmosphere of mistrust.
2. If a detector is in use, treat its output as a probability and not a finding. Over half of non-native-authored TOEFL essays in the study cited below were misclassified as AI generated by widely used detectors, while the same detectors were near perfect on US eighth grade essays.
3. _(Drafted)_ Never open with the score. Ask the student to walk through their process: how they got started, what they cut, where they got stuck.
4. _(Drafted)_ Ask for process evidence you can inspect together, such as drafts, notes, or a version history. If the course already requires a process artefact, check that first and often skip the conversation.
5. _(Drafted)_ Record what you did and why. If your institution requires the score to be reported, report it alongside your reservation rather than instead of it.


## Examples

Liang and colleagues evaluated seven widely used GPT detectors against writing from native and non- native English writers. More than half of the non-native-authored TOEFL essays were incorrectly classified as AI

generated, while accuracy on US eighth grade essays written by native speakers was near perfect. Analysis of the misclassified essays showed they had significantly lower perplexity than the rest, indicating that the detectors were responding to limited lexical variety rather than to machine authorship.

The same study demonstrated the evasion problem directly. Prompting a model to edit the non-native essays using more literary language substantially reduced misclassification, and simplifying native-written essays increased it. A detector that can be defeated by asking for better vocabulary is not a detector.

Pitt's guidance to instructors is to avoid generative AI detectors on the grounds that they are unreliable and create an atmosphere of mistrust, and to build a culture of honesty and transparency instead.

Corbin and colleagues make the structural version of the same point: because generative AI closely mimics human writing patterns, current detection tools are fraught with false positives and false negatives, so educators often have no feasible way to confirm whether a student complied with an AI use instruction.
