## Intent

Students learn to break a complex task into parts a model can handle and to iterate on a query rather than accepting the first response.


## Problem

Students are often assumed to be fluent with generative AI because they are fluent with technology generally. In practice they lack the specific skills of decomposing a task, engineering and refining a query, and recognizing where a model's output is unreliable. These are gaps students usually do not know they have, so they will not seek instruction in them.


## Solution

The instructor treats prompting as a teachable skill within the course rather than assumed background. Students practise decomposing a complex, course-relevant task into chunks a model can handle, iterating on queries to improve output, and identifying the limitations of what comes back, including bias and fabricated content. The focus is on students using AI well rather than on preventing misuse.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses where students will be permitted to use AI on substantive work, as preparation
+ Professional and pre-professional programs where students will encounter these tools in practice
+ Introductory courses, where establishing the skill early pays off across a program

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Specific prompting techniques date quickly as model interfaces and capabilities change
+ Class time spent on tool skills competes with disciplinary content
+ Risks reading as endorsement of AI use in courses where policy is more restrictive

Not a good fit when:

+ Course policy prohibits AI use, making the skill unusable in context


## How to Implement

1. Choose a complex course-relevant task rather than a generic one, so the decomposition practice is also disciplinary practice.
2. Have students break the task into chunks a model can manage.
3. Have students engineer an initial query, evaluate what comes back, and iterate for better output.
4. Direct students to identify the limitations in the results, including bias and hallucination.
5. _(Drafted)_ Debrief as a class on which decomposition strategies worked, so the skill is shared rather than discovered privately.


## Examples

The source's framing is that students need guidance in using generative AI effectively even though they may be assumed to have an instinctive knack for technology, and that these are skills students usually lack alongside knowledge gaps they do not know they face.

It names three specific skills to prompt students toward: decomposing complex tasks into AI-manageable chunks, engineering and iterating queries for better outputs, and recognising the limitations of generative AI including bias and hallucinations.

The source also states a position on detection tools relevant to this play's framing: it advises avoiding generative AI detectors on the grounds that they are unreliable and create an atmosphere of mistrust, and recommends building a culture of honesty and transparency instead.
