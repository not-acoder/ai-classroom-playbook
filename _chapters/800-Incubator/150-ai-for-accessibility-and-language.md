title: Permit AI Explicitly for Accessibility and Language Support
slug: ai-for-accessibility-and-language

## Intent

Students who need captioning, translation, or text simplification know these uses are permitted and are not weighing them against an integrity risk.


## Problem

Blanket AI restrictions fall unevenly. A student using a model for real-time captioning, translation of course materials, or simplification of dense reading is doing something categorically different from a student generating an essay, but a policy that does not distinguish them leaves the first student uncertain whether they are cheating. The cost lands on students with disabilities and multilingual students, who are least able to absorb it.


## Solution

The instructor names accessibility and language support uses as explicitly permitted in the course policy, separately from the general permission tier, and gives concrete examples so students do not have to interpret. The framing is that these uses fall under accessible course design rather than under integrity exceptions.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses with multilingual students or students using accommodations
+ Reading-heavy courses where simplification and summarization materially change access
+ Any course already writing an AI policy, since this is an addition rather than a separate document

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ The line between simplifying a reading and outsourcing engagement with it is genuinely blurry and will need judgment calls
+ Naming permitted uses invites edge cases the policy did not anticipate
+ Tool quality for translation and captioning varies by language, so the permission does not equalize access on its own

Not a good fit when:

+ Institutional accommodation processes already cover these uses and a course-level statement would conflict


## How to Implement

1. Identify which support uses are relevant to your course materials and student population.
2. Name them explicitly as permitted in your syllabus AI policy, separate from your general permission tier. The source's examples of uses that augment rather than replace learning include real-time captioning and text-to-speech for learners with disabilities, real-time translation of course materials, lectures, presentations and discussions for multilingual learners, and summarizing lecture notes and improving clarity of writing.
3. Distinguish these from uses that replace learning, which the source lists as including writing an essay in response to a prompt, generating answers to discussion questions, developing a research thesis, and solving assigned problems.
4. _(Drafted)_ Give students a concrete example of each side of the line for your own assignments, rather than leaving the categories abstract.
5. _(Drafted)_ Point students to a short resource on configuring the tools, since permission without setup guidance leaves the gap partly open.


## Examples

The source frames these capabilities as part of institutional efforts toward Universal Design for Learning, providing enhanced accessibility to course materials for learners with disabilities as well as multilingual learners. It names Microsoft Immersive Reader as a tool example for both text-to-speech with font adjustments and live translation for non-native speakers.

The source's augment-versus-replace table, which underpins {% include chapter-link.html slug="transparent-course-ai-policy" %}, places captioning, text-to-speech, summarizing, organizing information, and improving clarity or tone on the augmenting side.

The source is a policy and practice report rather than an instructor guide, so it does not supply syllabus language. Steps 4 and 5 exist to fill that gap and are drafted.
