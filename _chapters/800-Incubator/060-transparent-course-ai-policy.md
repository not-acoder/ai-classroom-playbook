## Intent

Students know, before beginning any assignment, which uses of AI are permitted in the course and what they are required to disclose.


## Problem

Students arrive already using generative AI, and without explicit course guidance they are left to guess where the line falls. Ambiguity produces both accidental integrity violations and students avoiding uses the instructor would have permitted. A single blanket rule does not fit every discipline: what counts as augmenting learning in a business course differs from what counts in a humanities writing seminar, and the same tool use can be appropriate in one and corrosive in the other.


## Solution

The instructor publishes a syllabus policy specifying which AI uses are permitted, which are restricted, and what disclosure is required. The policy is tiered rather than binary. One documented approach gives instructors three options to choose from per course: prohibit AI entirely, permit it for specified purposes with clear and transparent citation, or permit any use that augments rather than replaces learning, with citation. The augment-versus-replace distinction is drawn for the specific discipline rather than left abstract.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Any course, and particularly programs wanting consistency across multiple sections
+ Disciplines where the line between augmenting and replacing learning is discipline-specific and needs stating
+ Courses where the instructor also wants to model transparency about their own AI use

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Different policies across courses can confuse students unless there is a shared institutional vocabulary
+ Policies age quickly as tool capability changes and need revisiting
+ A tiered policy takes more thought to write than a blanket prohibition

Not a good fit when:

+ The institution mandates a single policy and does not permit instructor variation


## How to Implement

1. Decide the course's overall stance: prohibit AI, permit it for specified purposes with citation, or permit any augmenting use with citation.
2. Write out which specific uses fall on each side for your discipline. The source's augmenting list includes summarizing lecture notes, analyzing large datasets, brainstorming research ideas, generating an outline, organizing information, creating study guides, improving clarity or tone, and accessibility supports such as real-time captioning and text-to-speech. Its replacing list includes generating answers to discussion questions, developing a research thesis, solving assigned problems, writing an essay in response to a prompt, and generating images or other artifacts that constitute the assignment.
3. State what disclosure requires: when, how, and why a tool was used.
4. Set clear guidelines for AI use on individual assignments, and vary the policy by assignment type where useful.
5. Consider offering students a choice between AI and non-AI versions of an assignment.
6. Revisit the policy as new tools and capabilities emerge.


## Examples

The three-tier structure is in use at Dallas College and the University of South Carolina, where faculty choose one of three AI policies for each course they teach, described as a red light, yellow light, green light set of options.

Discipline-specific examples from the source: business courses may encourage AI for any task that creates efficiencies valued in a corporate environment; statistics courses may allow AI for analyzing large datasets and computer science courses may allow it as a round-the-clock tutor for difficult coding projects; humanities courses may prohibit AI entirely to ensure students perform the critical thinking and analysis that accompany reading and writing assignments.
