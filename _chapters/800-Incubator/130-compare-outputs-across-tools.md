## Intent

Students observe that different models answer the same prompt differently and articulate why the choice of tool matters.


## Problem

Students typically use one model and treat its output as the answer rather than as one answer. Without a comparison, there is nothing to reveal that the response was shaped by a particular system with particular limits, so tool choice never becomes a visible decision.


## Solution

The instructor assigns a task in which students submit the same prompt to two or more generative AI tools and compare the results. The comparison makes variation visible and turns tool selection into something students must reason about rather than default into. The assessed output is the student's reflection on the differences and on what the differences imply about choosing a tool for a given purpose.


## Applicability

Works well in:

+ Courses where evaluating sources and weighing competing accounts is already a skill being taught
+ Early-term AI literacy activities, before students are asked to use AI on substantive work
+ Disciplines where different tools have visibly different strengths, such as coding, writing, and quantitative reasoning

Tradeoffs and limitations:

+ Requires students to have access to more than one tool, which raises an equity question when the better tools are paid
+ Differences between current models are sometimes small enough that the comparison feels artificial
+ Results are not reproducible between students or across runs, so a common marking standard needs care

Not a good fit when:

+ Students have access to only one tool, or institutional policy permits only one


## How to Implement

1. Choose a course-relevant prompt where you expect model outputs to differ in a way students can detect.
2. Have students run the same prompt through at least two tools and record both outputs.
3. Ask students to identify the differences and account for them.
4. Ask students to reflect on what the comparison implies about the choices available to users and about the need to choose deliberately.
5. _(Drafted)_ Provide a fallback path for students without access to a second tool, such as supplying a second output yourself.


## Examples

The source recommends creating assignments in which students compare outputs from multiple generative AI tools, framing the goal as fostering reflection about the choices available to users and the need for users to choose wisely.

It pairs this with two adjacent moves: having students evaluate generative AI material for accuracy and bias problems, and asking students to reflect on the tool's suitability for different tasks so they become more sophisticated users.
